I reviewed the [live pilot](https://stoneham-fm.unitedmain.com/market) at desktop and a 400px mobile viewport, including vendor pages and the reservation form.

My verdict: it’s visually distinctive, warm, and ready for controlled pilot use. It already feels more like a local market than an e-commerce template. Before marketing it broadly, I’d address a few clarity and conversion issues.

## Highest-priority changes

1. **Put the location beside the date.**  
   A first-time visitor can learn when the market happens, but not precisely where. Add:

   `Stoneham Town Common · 340 Main St · Get directions`

   The current official market information lists the Town Common at 340 Main Street. [Stoneham Farmers Market information](https://www.stonehamcan.org/farmersmarket)

2. **Make market status dynamic.**  
   At 3:39pm on July 30—during market hours—the hero still said “Next market: July 30.” That is a missed excitement opportunity. Use states such as:

   - “Happening now · Open until 6:30”
   - “Tomorrow afternoon · 2:30–6:30”
   - “Next Thursday · August 6”

3. **Resolve the reservation contradiction.**  
   The page says “we’ll hold it for you,” but later warns that pickup is not guaranteed if items sell out. That undermines the main value proposition. Establish a precise policy, for example:

   > Confirmed reservations are held until 5:45pm. After that, availability may change.

   Repeat the market date, location, hold-until time, and payment expectation inside the reservation form.

4. **Shorten the market page.**  
   Showing every product from every vendor creates a very long mobile journey and eventually feels more like a catalog. On the main page, show each vendor’s story plus two or three featured products, followed by “See everything Riverdale is bringing.” Keep the full assortment on vendor pages.

5. **Use the desktop canvas more confidently.**  
   Desktop product content remains in a roughly mobile-width column with large empty gutters. At wider breakpoints, consider:

   - A two-column vendor introduction
   - Two-column product layouts
   - Alternating editorial compositions
   - A sticky “This week” vendor index

   The current narrow composition works much better on mobile than desktop.

## Mobile findings

The mobile hero is strong: the headline, date, CTA, and vendor links fit together cleanly, while the imagery makes the market feel tangible.

The main improvements:

- Reserve buttons appear approximately 30px high; target at least 44px for comfortable tapping.
- Several body and fine-print styles are small and low-contrast, especially the pilot disclaimer.
- The reservation sheet has only a drag handle. Add an explicit close button—Escape works on desktop, but phone users need a visible exit.
- The confirmation button sits at the bottom of an internally scrolling sheet. A sticky action area with safe-area padding would be more dependable.
- Repeated accessible labels such as “Reserve for pickup” need the product name: “Reserve Broccoli from Riverdale Farm.”

## What is already working

- The navy, gold, serif typography, and real photography create a recognizable identity.
- The hero clearly prioritizes vendor discovery.
- Vendor stories are concrete and local.
- “Reserve for pickup” and “No payment now” are easy to understand.
- The empty vendor state is charming without becoming silly.
- The reservation sheet dismisses with Escape and correctly returns keyboard focus.
- The vendor jump links are useful on a page this long.

## Make it more exciting

“This week at the market” currently functions mainly as a vendor directory. Turn it into the editorial heartbeat of the page:

- “Peaches are at their peak”
- “New this week: Del Sur’s New Mexico Chicken”
- “Ask Wilmington Honey Bee about spring versus fall honey”
- “Three things worth arriving early for”

That will answer “Why should I go this week?” rather than merely “Who is listed?”

## How to validate with limited traffic

Don’t A/B test yet. Run five short tests with local shoppers on their own phones:

1. Where and when is the market?
2. Find something you would want and reserve it.
3. Explain what happens after reserving.
4. Find one reason to visit a particular booth.

Track just five events: vendor-list CTA, vendor selection, reservation opened, reservation completed, and directions clicked. Those will expose the biggest drop-offs without building a heavy analytics system.

Overall: preserve the current visual personality. The best next step is not a redesign—it’s tightening location, urgency, reservation trust, tap ergonomics, and desktop composition.
